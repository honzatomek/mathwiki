\chapter[Boundary Conditions]{Boundary Conditions and Single-DOF Constraints}\label{chap:boundary-conditions}

A finite element model is not defined by its elements alone. The governing
field equations admit many possible solutions until sufficient information is
provided at the boundary, at supports and, for transient problems, at the
initial time. In engineering language this information may be called a fixed
support, roller, imposed displacement, pressure, elastic foundation or base
motion. In mathematical language the same definitions appear under names such
as \textit{Dirichlet}, \textit{Neumann}, \textit{Robin}, \textit{holonomic} or
\textit{rheonomic}. The terminology can sound more exotic than the underlying
engineering idea.

This chapter starts from the simplest case: one prescribed discrete degree of
freedom (DOF). It then develops directional or \textit{skew} constraints, where
one scalar motion is prescribed in a local direction that is not aligned with
the global coordinate system. General relations involving several independent
DOFs are postponed to Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}; unilateral
contact conditions are treated in Chapter~\ref{chap:contact-formulations}; and
rigid, interpolating and remote couplings are developed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:kinematic-constraints}.

As in the following chapters, the same idea will be viewed at three levels:

\begin{center}
\textbf{engineering meaning}
$\longleftrightarrow$
\textbf{mathematical formulation}
$\longleftrightarrow$
\textbf{discrete algorithm}.
\end{center}

The first tells us what the support or load means physically. The second makes
work conjugacy, admissible motion and linearization visible. The third shows
what the finite element program must actually index, transform, eliminate and
assemble.

\section{What Is a Boundary Condition?}

Consider the displacement form of the structural equilibrium equations in a
domain $\Omega$ with boundary $\Gamma$,

\begin{equation}
    \nabla\!\cdot\m{\sigma}+\m{b}=\rho\ddot{\m{u}}
    \qquad \text{in }\Omega.
\end{equation}

For the standard displacement formulation, two fundamental kinds of boundary
data appear:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc-dirichlet-continuum}
    \m{u}=\closure{\m{u}}
    \qquad \text{on }\Gamma_u,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc-neumann-continuum}
    \m{\sigma}\m{n}=\closure{\m{t}}
    \qquad \text{on }\Gamma_t.
\end{equation}

The first prescribes the primary solution variable itself; the second prescribes
its work-conjugate boundary traction. Normally
$\Gamma_u\cup\Gamma_t=\Gamma$ and the two parts do not overlap except at their
boundaries.

    \caption{Engineering interpretation of the classical boundary-condition
    names for displacement-based structural mechanics. Dirichlet prescribes

\end{figure}

\subsection{Dirichlet or Essential Boundary Conditions}

A \textit{Dirichlet} boundary condition prescribes the value of the primary
unknown. In structural mechanics this usually means displacement or rotation,
for example

\begin{equation}
    u_x=0,
    \qquad
    u_y=2\ \mathrm{mm},
    \qquad
    \varphi_z=0.
\end{equation}

The term \textit{essential} comes from the variational formulation: the trial
and test spaces must satisfy these kinematic restrictions. In everyday
engineering language this is simply a \textbf{support or imposed motion}.

A zero prescribed value is called \textit{homogeneous},

\begin{equation}
    u_i=0,
\end{equation}

while a nonzero value is \textit{nonhomogeneous},

\begin{equation}
    u_i=\closure{u}_i.
\end{equation}

The mathematical name therefore does not imply a special type of physical
support. A fixed support and a prescribed actuator stroke are both Dirichlet
conditions; only the prescribed value differs.

\subsection{Neumann or Natural Boundary Conditions}

A \textit{Neumann} boundary condition prescribes the work-conjugate flux. For
structural mechanics this is traction,

\begin{equation}
    \m{t}=\m{\sigma}\m{n}=\closure{\m{t}},
\end{equation}

which generates the finite element load contribution

\begin{equation}\label{eq:neumann-load-vector}
    \m{f}_{\Gamma}
    =
    \int_{\Gamma_t}\m{N}^{T}\closure{\m{t}}\,d\Gamma.
\end{equation}

In engineering language these are \textbf{forces, moments, tractions and
pressures}. They are called \textit{natural} because they arise automatically
from integration by parts in the weak form and enter the right-hand side of the
finite element equations.

A mechanically free surface usually means the homogeneous natural condition

\begin{equation}
    \m{\sigma}\m{n}=\m{0}.
\end{equation}

Thus ``no explicitly applied boundary load'' is itself the zero-Neumann case;
it is not the same statement as ``no boundary condition exists.''

\subsection{Robin Boundary Conditions and Elastic Supports}

A \textit{Robin} condition relates the primary variable to its conjugate flux.
A simple structural example is an elastic support,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:robin-structural}
    t+k\left(u-u_0\right)=\closure{t},
\end{equation}

where $k$ is a boundary stiffness and $u_0$ is the support reference position.
After discretization, a distributed version contributes both stiffness and
load,

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{K}_{\Gamma}
    &=&
    \int_{\Gamma}\m{N}^{T}k\m{N}\,d\Gamma,\\
    \m{f}_{\Gamma}
    &=&
    \int_{\Gamma}\m{N}^{T}\left(\closure{t}+ku_0\right)\,d\Gamma.
\end{eqarray}

In engineering language this is a \textbf{spring, foundation, impedance or
elastic support}. It does not prescribe the displacement exactly; instead the
reaction depends on the displacement that the solution produces.

The word \textit{mixed} is unfortunately used in two ways. It may refer to a
Robin relation that mixes value and flux, or simply to a boundary-value problem
where different portions of the boundary carry different condition types. The
meaning should therefore be inferred from context rather than from the word
alone.

\begin{bbox}
\textbf{Engineering note -- The names belong to the formulation, not to the
hardware.}

For the standard displacement-based structural formulation, displacement is an
essential/Dirichlet quantity and traction is a natural/Neumann quantity. In a
mixed finite element formulation with additional primary fields, the same
physical quantity may enter the mathematics differently. The engineering
support does not change; the choice of primary unknowns does.
\end{bbox}

\subsection{Initial Conditions Are a Different Requirement}

A transient problem also needs an initial state, for example

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}(\m{x},0)=\m{u}_0(\m{x}),
    \qquad
    \dot{\m{u}}(\m{x},0)=\m{v}_0(\m{x}).
\end{equation}

These are \textit{initial conditions}, not spatial boundary conditions. A base
motion $\closure{u}(t)$ may be time-dependent boundary data, while
$u(0),\dot u(0)$ define the starting state of the evolution problem.

\section{Constraint Classifications}

Terms such as Dirichlet and Neumann answer a different question from terms such
as holonomic or unilateral. The latter describe the nature of the admissible
motion itself. Several labels may therefore apply to the same engineering
constraint at once.

\subsection{Holonomic Constraints}

A constraint is \textit{holonomic} if it can be written as a relation between
configuration variables and, possibly, time,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:holonomic-constraint}
    c\left(\m{q},t\right)=0.
\end{equation}

Examples include a prescribed displacement

\begin{equation}
    q_i-\closure{q}_i(t)=0,
\end{equation}

and a fixed distance between two points,

\begin{equation}
    \left(\m{x}_2-\m{x}_1\right)^T
    \left(\m{x}_2-\m{x}_1\right)-L^2=0.
\end{equation}

Most ordinary structural supports, rigid links and joint relations used in
finite element statics are holonomic. The term sounds abstract, but the
engineering translation is simple:

\begin{center}
\textit{the admissible geometry can be described directly from the current
configuration.}
\end{center}

\subsection{Non-Holonomic Constraints}

A \textit{non-holonomic} constraint generally involves velocities and cannot be
integrated into a configuration-only relation of the form
\eqref{eq:holonomic-constraint}. A classical example is the no-lateral-slip
condition of a rolling wheel with heading angle $\psi$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonholonomic-wheel}
    -\sin\psi\,\dot{x}
    +\cos\psi\,\dot{y}=0.
\end{equation}

The wheel may move in the direction in which it points but may not have an
instantaneous velocity perpendicular to that direction. Because the allowed
direction itself changes with $\psi$, the condition is not in general reducible
to one fixed relation among $x$, $y$ and $\psi$.

Non-holonomic constraints are common in multibody dynamics, vehicle models and
robotics but much less common in conventional structural finite element statics.
They are mentioned here mainly to prevent the word \textit{constraint} from
being interpreted as synonymous with a displacement equation.

\subsection{Scleronomic and Rheonomic Constraints}

A holonomic constraint with no explicit time dependence,

\begin{equation}
    c(\m{q})=0,
\end{equation}

is called \textit{scleronomic}. A constraint with an explicit prescribed time
law,

\begin{equation}
    c(\m{q},t)=0,
\end{equation}

is called \textit{rheonomic}. Engineering examples are respectively a fixed
support and a prescribed shaker/base displacement. The intimidating terminology
therefore amounts to the practical distinction

\begin{center}
\textbf{fixed in time} $\quad$ versus $\quad$ \textbf{driven by a time law}.
\end{center}

\subsection{Bilateral and Unilateral Constraints}

A bilateral equality constraint remains active in both directions,

\begin{equation}
    c(\m{q})=0.
\end{equation}

A unilateral constraint restricts only one side of the admissible domain, for
example the contact gap

\begin{equation}
    g_n(\m{q})\ge0.
\end{equation}

The latter requires an active-set or equivalent state logic and is the subject
of Chapter~\ref{chap:contact-formulations}. A fixed support is therefore a
bilateral holonomic constraint, while frictionless contact is unilateral and
state-dependent.

\subsection{Linear and Nonlinear Constraints}

A simple support component

\begin{equation}
    u_x=0
\end{equation}

is linear in the discrete unknowns. A fixed-distance relation is nonlinear in
Cartesian coordinates. This distinction controls whether the constraint
coefficients can be built once or must be updated during nonlinear iterations.
The same idea reappears in Chapters~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints} and
\ref{chap:kinematic-constraints}.

\begin{bbox}
\textbf{Engineering note -- Classification is multi-dimensional.}

A prescribed sinusoidal base displacement is simultaneously Dirichlet,
essential, holonomic, bilateral, rheonomic and possibly linear. These words are
not competing names for one list of boundary-condition types; they answer
different questions about the same definition.
\end{bbox}

\section{Single-DOF Essential Constraints}\label{sec:single-dof-bc}

After discretization, the simplest essential boundary condition prescribes one
scalar degree of freedom,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:single-dof-constraint}
    u_p=\closure{u}_p.
\end{equation}

The DOF may be a translation, rotation, temperature, electric potential or any
other primary nodal unknown. In structural solver terminology such definitions
are often called \textit{single-point constraints} (SPCs).

\subsection{Partitioned Equilibrium Equations}

For static structural equilibrium,

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}\m{u}=\m{f},
\end{equation}

partition the unknowns into free $f$ and prescribed $p$ components,

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{K}_{ff} & \m{K}_{fp}\\
        \m{K}_{pf} & \m{K}_{pp}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_f\\
        \closure{\m{u}}_p
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{f}_f\\
        \m{f}_p
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The free equations become

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdof-reduced-equilibrium}
    \boxed{
    \m{K}_{ff}\m{u}_f
    =
    \m{f}_f-\m{K}_{fp}\closure{\m{u}}_p
    }.
\end{equation}

The support reactions are recovered only after the displacement solution is
known,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdof-reaction}
    \boxed{
    \m{r}_p
    =
    \m{K}_{pf}\m{u}_f
    +\m{K}_{pp}\closure{\m{u}}_p
    -\m{f}_p
    }.
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:sdof-reduced-equilibrium} contains an important practical
point: a nonzero prescribed displacement generates an equivalent load on the
free equations through $-\m{K}_{fp}\closure{\m{u}}_p$. It is therefore not
correct to ``set the displacement after solving.'' The prescribed motion must
participate in the equilibrium equations.

\subsection{Worked Example: Two-Node Spring}

Consider a spring of stiffness $k=1000~\mathrm{N/mm}$,

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1000&-1000\\
        -1000&1000
    \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/mm},
    \qquad
    \m{f}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0\\100
    \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N}.
\end{equation}

Prescribing $u_1=0$ leaves

\begin{equation}
    1000u_2=100,
\end{equation}

so

\begin{equation}
    u_2=0.1~\mathrm{mm}.
\end{equation}

The reaction follows from the original equation,

\begin{equation}
    r_1=-100~\mathrm{N}.
\end{equation}

The following reusable implementation constructs the reduced system from an
arbitrary list of prescribed global DOF indices. The matrix is not altered in
place; instead the free/prescribed partitions are returned explicitly.

\begin{python}
import numpy as np


def solve_dense(A, b):
    """Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting."""
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float, copy=True)
    b = np.array(b, dtype=float, copy=True)
    n = len(b)

    for k in range(n - 1):
        pivot = k
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            if abs(A[i, k]) > abs(A[pivot, k]):
                pivot = i
        if abs(A[pivot, k]) < 1.0e-14:
            raise ValueError("singular matrix")
        if pivot != k:
            A[[k, pivot]] = A[[pivot, k]]
            b[[k, pivot]] = b[[pivot, k]]

        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            factor = A[i, k] / A[k, k]
            A[i, k:] -= factor * A[k, k:]
            b[i] -= factor * b[k]

    x = np.zeros(n, dtype=float)
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        rhs = b[i]
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            rhs -= A[i, j] * x[j]
        x[i] = rhs / A[i, i]
    return x


def reduce_dirichlet(K, f, prescribed):
    """
    prescribed = [(global_dof_index, value), ...]
    """
    n = len(f)
    prescribed_ids = [int(item[0]) for item in prescribed]
    prescribed_values = np.array([item[1] for item in prescribed], dtype=float)

    free_ids = []
    for i in range(n):
        if i not in prescribed_ids:
            free_ids.append(i)

    Kff = K[np.ix_(free_ids, free_ids)]
    Kfp = K[np.ix_(free_ids, prescribed_ids)]
    ff = f[free_ids] - Kfp @ prescribed_values
    return Kff, ff, free_ids, prescribed_ids, prescribed_values


def recover_dirichlet_solution(K, f, reduced):
    Kff, ff, free_ids, prescribed_ids, prescribed_values = reduced
    uf = solve_dense(Kff, ff)

    u = np.zeros(len(f), dtype=float)
    u[free_ids] = uf
    u[prescribed_ids] = prescribed_values
    reactions = K @ u - f
    return u, reactions


K = np.array([[1000.0, -1000.0],
              [-1000.0, 1000.0]])
f = np.array([0.0, 100.0])

reduced = reduce_dirichlet(K, f, prescribed=[(0, 0.0)])
u, reactions = recover_dirichlet_solution(K, f, reduced)

print("u =", u)
print("reaction at prescribed DOF =", reactions[0])
\end{python}

The code makes the continuum phrase ``impose a Dirichlet boundary condition''
concrete. The program must identify equation numbers, create free and prescribed
index sets, form the reduced matrix and effective right-hand side, solve the
free equations, scatter the prescribed values back and finally recover the
reaction from the original equilibrium equations.

\subsection{Alternative Numerical Treatments}

Direct elimination is not the only way to impose
\eqref{eq:single-dof-constraint}. A solver may modify matrix rows and columns,
use a penalty coefficient, introduce a Lagrange multiplier or internally build
a transformation. These alternatives are developed for the general case in
Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}. For a simple SDOF boundary
condition, elimination is usually the clearest reference formulation because it
removes exactly the inadmissible motion without introducing an artificial
stiffness parameter.

\section{Natural Loads and Elastic Boundary Supports}

The discrete distinction between an imposed motion and an applied load is worth
making explicit. A nodal Neumann load simply contributes to the right-hand side,

\begin{equation}
    f_i\eqp F_i,
\end{equation}

whereas a linear elastic Robin support contributes both stiffness and load. For
a scalar spring of stiffness $k_s$ connected to a prescribed support position
$u_0$,

\begin{equation}
    f_s=-k_s(u-u_0),
\end{equation}

so the equilibrium equations receive

\begin{equation}
    K_{ii}\eqp k_s,
    \qquad
    f_i\eqp k_su_0.
\end{equation}

The implementation is correspondingly simple:

\begin{python}
def add_boundary_spring(K, f, dof, stiffness, reference_displacement=0.0):
    """Add one scalar Robin/elastic-support contribution."""
    K[dof, dof] += stiffness
    f[dof] += stiffness * reference_displacement


K = np.zeros((2, 2), dtype=float)
f = np.zeros(2, dtype=float)

add_boundary_spring(K, f, dof=1, stiffness=500.0,
                    reference_displacement=0.2)

print(K)
print(f)
\end{python}

The spring is therefore not a ``soft Dirichlet condition'' in a mathematical
sense; it is an additional constitutive relation that stores energy and changes
the structural stiffness. Only in the limiting penalty-like case of an
extremely large stiffness does it approximate a prescribed displacement, with
the usual conditioning consequences.

\section{Directional or Skew Single-DOF Constraints}\label{sec:skew-boundary-conditions}

A roller support is the simplest example of a boundary condition that is easy to
describe physically but awkward if one insists on global Cartesian components.
The support restrains motion normal to the supporting surface while allowing
tangential motion. It is still only \textbf{one scalar prescribed DOF}; that DOF
simply belongs to a local basis.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{img/bc_skew_support.png}
    \caption{A skew roller support. In the local $n$--$t$ basis only the normal
    displacement is constrained. Written in global $x$--$y$ components, the
    same scalar condition contains two displacement coefficients.}
    \label{fig:bc-skew-support}
\end{figure}

Let the unit normal and tangent directions in two dimensions be

\begin{equation}
    \m{n}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        n_x\\n_y
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \m{t}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -n_y\\n_x
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The local displacement components are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:skew-local-transform}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u_n\\u_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{
    \begin{bmatrix}
        n_x&n_y\\
        -n_y&n_x
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\m{C}_{lg}}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u_x\\u_y
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

A normal roller condition is simply

\begin{equation}\label{eq:skew-normal-constraint}
    u_n=\closure{u}_n.
\end{equation}

In global components the same relation is

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    n_xu_x+n_yu_y=\closure{u}_n
    }.
\end{equation}

This is an important bridge to Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}:
what is a single-DOF condition in a rotated nodal basis becomes a two-coefficient
constraint equation when written directly in the global basis.

\subsection{Transforming the Equilibrium Equations}

From \eqref{eq:skew-local-transform},

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_l=\m{C}_{lg}\m{u}_g,
    \qquad
    \m{u}_g=\m{C}_{lg}^{T}\m{u}_l,
\end{equation}

because $\m{C}_{lg}$ is orthonormal. With

\begin{equation}
    \m{T}=\m{C}_{lg}^{T},
    \qquad
    \m{u}_g=\m{T}\m{u}_l,
\end{equation}

work preservation gives

\begin{eqarray}\label{eq:skew-transformed-system}
    \m{K}_l&=&\m{T}^{T}\m{K}_g\m{T},\\
    \m{M}_l&=&\m{T}^{T}\m{M}_g\m{T},\\
    \m{C}_l&=&\m{T}^{T}\m{C}_g\m{T},\\
    \m{f}_l&=&\m{T}^{T}\m{f}_g.
\end{eqarray}

Only the rows and columns associated with nodes whose basis is changed need to
be transformed. Depending on the solver architecture, this may be performed on
an element contribution before global assembly, as a nodal transformation of
the assembled system, or by generating an equivalent MFC row. None of these is
a universal requirement of the formulation.

The same operation can be written directly for the complete dynamic equilibrium
system. Starting from

\begin{equation}
    \m{M}\ddot{\m{u}}_g
    +\m{C}\dot{\m{u}}_g
    +\m{K}\m{u}_g
    =\m{f}_g,
\end{equation}

and substituting $\m{u}_g=\m{T}\m{u}_l$ gives

\begin{equation}
    \closure{\m{M}}\ddot{\m{u}}_l
    +\closure{\m{C}}\dot{\m{u}}_l
    +\closure{\m{K}}\m{u}_l
    =\closure{\m{f}},
\end{equation}

with

\begin{eqarray}
    \closure{\m{M}}&=&\m{T}^T\m{M}\m{T},\\
    \closure{\m{C}}&=&\m{T}^T\m{C}\m{T},\\
    \closure{\m{K}}&=&\m{T}^T\m{K}\m{T},\\
    \closure{\m{f}}&=&\m{T}^T\m{f}.
\end{eqarray}

Although these expressions are written as transformations of the complete
matrices, the actual arithmetic needs to touch only the affected nodal rows and
columns. In an assembly-oriented implementation it is often convenient to
transform the corresponding element contributions immediately before they are
scattered into the global arrays.

\begin{notebooknote}[Notebook note -- A skew support does not require rotating the whole model]
This implementation detail is easy to miss when looking only at the compact
congruence transformation. Only the DOFs
whose basis changes are affected. A code can rotate the relevant element rows
and columns immediately before assembly, transform an assembled nodal block, or
replace the operation by an equivalent MFC. The mathematical operation is local
even if it is written with full global matrices.
\end{notebooknote}

\subsection{Angle-Defined Cartesian Basis in Two Dimensions}

For a planar model a local Cartesian system may be specified directly by an
angle $\alpha$. If $(u,w)$ are the in-plane translations and $\psi$ is the
out-of-plane small rotation, then

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u_l\\
        w_l\\
        \psi_l
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \phantom{-}\cos\alpha&\sin\alpha&0\\
        -\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\
        0&0&1
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\m{C}_{lg}}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u_g\\
        w_g\\
        \psi_g
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The scalar rotation about the common out-of-plane axis is unchanged. Thus a
planar node with a rotated translational support does not require any special
rotation formula beyond the ordinary two-dimensional basis transformation.

\subsection{Worked Example: Roller at 30 Degrees}

Consider a two-component nodal stiffness and load

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_g
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1200&300\\
        300&800
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \m{f}_g
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        100\\50
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Let the support tangent make $30^\circ$ with the global $x$ axis. Then

\begin{equation}
    \m{t}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos30^\circ\\\sin30^\circ
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \m{n}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -\sin30^\circ\\\cos30^\circ
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Using the local ordering $(u_n,u_t)$ gives approximately

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_l
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        640.192&-23.205\\
        -23.205&1359.808
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \m{f}_l
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -6.699\\111.603
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

With $u_n=0$, the one remaining equation gives

\begin{equation}
    u_t=0.08207,
\end{equation}

and transformation back to global components gives

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_g
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.07108\\0.04104
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The support has therefore removed only the motion normal to the roller while
retaining tangential compliance.

The following function constructs the basis and performs the same operation:

\begin{python}
def normalize2(v):
    length = np.sqrt(v[0] * v[0] + v[1] * v[1])
    if length < 1.0e-14:
        raise ValueError("zero direction vector")
    return np.array([v[0] / length, v[1] / length])


def skew_basis_2d(normal):
    n = normalize2(np.array(normal, dtype=float))
    t = np.array([n[1], -n[0]])
    Q = np.array([[n[0], n[1]],
                  [t[0], t[1]]])
    return Q


def solve_skew_node(K_global, f_global, normal,
                    prescribed_normal=0.0):
    Q = skew_basis_2d(normal)       # local = Q @ global
    T = Q.T                         # global = T @ local
    K_local = T.T @ K_global @ T
    f_local = T.T @ f_global

    # Local DOF 0 is prescribed; local DOF 1 remains free.
    rhs_t = f_local[1] - K_local[1, 0] * prescribed_normal
    u_t = rhs_t / K_local[1, 1]

    u_local = np.array([prescribed_normal, u_t])
    u_global = T @ u_local
    reaction_local = K_local @ u_local - f_local
    return u_global, u_local, reaction_local, K_local, f_local


angle = np.deg2rad(30.0)
normal = np.array([-np.sin(angle), np.cos(angle)])
K_global = np.array([[1200.0, 300.0],
                     [300.0, 800.0]])
f_global = np.array([100.0, 50.0])

u_global, u_local, reaction_local, K_local, f_local = \
    solve_skew_node(K_global, f_global, normal)

print("u_local =", u_local)
print("u_global =", u_global)
print("normal reaction =", reaction_local[0])
\end{python}

\begin{bbox}
\textbf{Engineering note -- Skew does not automatically mean multi-DOF.}

A roller with one restrained local direction is a one-DOF essential condition
from the engineering viewpoint. It becomes a multi-coefficient equation only if
we insist on expressing it in the global basis. This distinction is useful when
reading solver input: a rotated nodal coordinate system and an explicit MPC may
represent exactly the same linear kinematics by different numerical routes.
\end{bbox}

\section{Three-Dimensional Local Cartesian Bases}

For a three-dimensional node, a local Cartesian basis should be orthonormal.
Simply normalizing two arbitrary user vectors independently is not sufficient,
because those vectors need not be perpendicular. A robust construction uses a
Gram--Schmidt step.

Let $\m{a}$ define the desired local $x$ direction and $\m{b}$ approximately
define the local $xy$ plane. Then

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{e}_x
    &=&
    \frac{\m{a}}{\norm{\m{a}}},\\
    \m{b}_{\perp}
    &=&
    \m{b}-\left(\m{e}_x^T\m{b}\right)\m{e}_x,\\
    \m{e}_y
    &=&
    \frac{\m{b}_{\perp}}{\norm{\m{b}_{\perp}}},\\
    \m{e}_z
    &=&
    \m{e}_x\times\m{e}_y.
\end{eqarray}

The global-to-local transformation is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cartesian-local-Q}
    \m{C}_{lg}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{e}_x^T\\
        \m{e}_y^T\\
        \m{e}_z^T
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \m{u}_l=\m{C}_{lg}\m{u}_g.
\end{equation}

For a node carrying three translations and three small rotations, the same
basis change is used for both vector blocks,

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_6
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{C}_{lg}&\m{0}\\
        \m{0}&\m{C}_{lg}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}


Written out explicitly,

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
 u_l\\v_l\\w_l\\\phi_l\\\psi_l\\\rho_l
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
 e_{x1}&e_{x2}&e_{x3}&0&0&0\\
 e_{y1}&e_{y2}&e_{y3}&0&0&0\\
 e_{z1}&e_{z2}&e_{z3}&0&0&0\\
 0&0&0&e_{x1}&e_{x2}&e_{x3}\\
 0&0&0&e_{y1}&e_{y2}&e_{y3}\\
 0&0&0&e_{z1}&e_{z2}&e_{z3}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
 u_g\\v_g\\w_g\\\phi_g\\\psi_g\\\rho_g
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Definition by an Origin and Two Points}

A common engineering definition uses an origin

\begin{equation}
    \m{O}=\begin{bmatrix}x_O&y_O&z_O\end{bmatrix}^T,
\end{equation}

a point $\m{P}_x$ on the desired local $x$ axis and a point $\m{P}_{xy}$ in
the desired local $xy$ plane. The two defining vectors are

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{a}
    &=&
    \m{P}_x-\m{O}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{P_x}-x_O\\y_{P_x}-y_O\\z_{P_x}-z_O
    \end{bmatrix},\\
    \m{b}
    &=&
    \m{P}_{xy}-\m{O}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{P_{xy}}-x_O\\y_{P_{xy}}-y_O\\z_{P_{xy}}-z_O
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{eqarray}

The local basis then follows from the same orthogonalization used above,

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{e}_x&=&\frac{\m{a}}{\norm{\m{a}}},\\
    \widehat{\m{e}}_y&=&
        \m{b}-(\m{e}_x^T\m{b})\m{e}_x,\\
    \m{e}_y&=&\frac{\widehat{\m{e}}_y}{\norm{\widehat{\m{e}}_y}},\\
    \m{e}_z&=&\m{e}_x\times\m{e}_y.
\end{eqarray}

This construction also shows why an origin plus two arbitrary independently
normalized vectors is not sufficient: the second direction must be projected
onto the plane perpendicular to the first before normalization.

For a strictly planar model only one in-plane direction is needed. With

\begin{equation}
    \m{e}_z=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\end{bmatrix}^T,
\end{equation}

one may construct the remaining direction by

\begin{equation}
    \m{e}_y=\m{e}_z\times\m{e}_x,
\end{equation}

and the right-handed closure relations are

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{e}_x&=&\m{e}_y\times\m{e}_z,\\
    \m{e}_y&=&\m{e}_z\times\m{e}_x,\\
    \m{e}_z&=&\m{e}_x\times\m{e}_y.
\end{eqarray}

The detailed theory of coordinate transformations is developed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:coordinate-transformations}; here the matrix is used only to
define the local directions in which boundary conditions are applied.

\begin{python}
def dot3(a, b):
    value = 0.0
    for i in range(3):
        value += a[i] * b[i]
    return value


def cross3(a, b):
    return np.array([
        a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1],
        a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2],
        a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0],
    ])


def normalize3(v):
    length = np.sqrt(dot3(v, v))
    if length < 1.0e-14:
        raise ValueError("zero or degenerate direction")
    return v / length


def cartesian_basis_3d(x_direction, xy_direction):
    ex = normalize3(np.array(x_direction, dtype=float))
    b = np.array(xy_direction, dtype=float)
    b_perp = b - dot3(ex, b) * ex
    ey = normalize3(b_perp)
    ez = normalize3(cross3(ex, ey))
    return np.vstack([ex, ey, ez])


def six_dof_basis(Q):
    Q6 = np.zeros((6, 6), dtype=float)
    Q6[0:3, 0:3] = Q
    Q6[3:6, 3:6] = Q
    return Q6


Q = cartesian_basis_3d(
    x_direction=[1.0, 1.0, 0.0],
    xy_direction=[0.0, 1.0, 1.0],
)
Q6 = six_dof_basis(Q)

print(Q)
print(Q @ Q.T)   # identity to roundoff
\end{python}

\section{Cylindrical Boundary Bases}

A cylindrical boundary condition is often misunderstood as a transformation of
the displacement vector into the coordinate values $(r,\varphi,z)$. For
small displacement structural analysis the practical operation is simpler: at
each node, construct a local Cartesian basis whose axes point in the radial,
tangential and axial directions, and impose ordinary local DOF constraints in
that basis.

\begin{notebooknote}[Notebook note -- A cylindrical support is a node-by-node skew Cartesian support]
For small-displacement structural work this is the most useful mental model: the solver does not need a special ``radial displacement
variable''. It needs an ordinary local Cartesian frame at each node, with local
$x$ chosen as $\m{e}_r$, local $y$ as $\m{e}_{\varphi}$ and local $z$ along the
cylinder axis. The only unusual feature is that this Cartesian basis is different
at every circumferential node.
\end{notebooknote}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.69\textwidth]{img/bc_cylindrical_basis.png}
    \caption{A cylindrical boundary basis is position-dependent. Each node on
    the circumference has a different radial and tangential direction even
    though all nodes refer to the same cylindrical coordinate system.}
    \label{fig:bc-cylindrical-basis}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Cylindrical Point Coordinates}

The coordinate values $(r,\varphi,z)$ and the local vector basis
$(\m{e}_r,\m{e}_{\varphi},\m{e}_z)$ should be kept conceptually separate. The
former describe where a point is; the latter describe the directions in which a
vector such as displacement, force or reaction is resolved.

When a Cartesian and cylindrical coordinate system share the same origin and
$z$ axis,

\begin{eqarray}
    x&=&r\cos\varphi,\\
    y&=&r\sin\varphi,\\
    z&=&z,
\end{eqarray}

and inversely

\begin{eqarray}
    r&=&\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\
    \varphi&=&\atantwo(y,x).
\end{eqarray}

The azimuth is indeterminate at $x=y=0$. An explicit principal-value
reconstruction from $\arcsin$ is

\begin{equation}
\varphi=
\begin{cases}
 \phantom{-}\arcsin(y/r), & x>0,\\
 \pi-\arcsin(y/r), & x<0,\;y\ge0,\\
 -\pi-\arcsin(y/r), & x<0,\;y<0,\\
 \phantom{-}\pi/2, & x=0,\;y>0,\\
 -\pi/2, & x=0,\;y<0,\\
 \text{indeterminate}, & x=0,\;y=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, the familiar quadrant-aware arctangent can be written

\begin{equation}
\atantwo(y,x)=
\begin{cases}
 \arctan(y/x), & x>0,\\
 \arctan(y/x)+\pi, & x<0,\;y\ge0,\\
 \arctan(y/x)-\pi, & x<0,\;y<0,\\
 \phantom{-}\pi/2, & x=0,\;y>0,\\
 -\pi/2, & x=0,\;y<0,\\
 \text{indeterminate}, & x=0,\;y=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This is the reason numerical libraries provide a two-argument
$\atantwo(y,x)$ rather than requiring the user to reconstruct the quadrant from
$\arctan(y/x)$.

\subsection{A General Cylindrical Coordinate System Defined by Three Points}

Let the cylindrical system be specified by an origin $\m{x}_O^c$, a point
$\m{x}_R^c$ on its reference radial axis and a point $\m{x}_{RZ}^c$ in its
reference $rz$ plane. Define

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{e}_{r0}
    &=&
    \frac{\m{x}_R^c-\m{x}_O^c}
         {\norm{\m{x}_R^c-\m{x}_O^c}},\\
    \widehat{\m{e}}_z
    &=&
    \frac{\m{x}_{RZ}^c-\m{x}_O^c}
         {\norm{\m{x}_{RZ}^c-\m{x}_O^c}},\\
    \m{e}_{y0}
    &=&
    \frac{\widehat{\m{e}}_z\times\m{e}_{r0}}
         {\norm{\widehat{\m{e}}_z\times\m{e}_{r0}}},\\
    \m{e}_{z0}
    &=&
    \m{e}_{r0}\times\m{e}_{y0}.
\end{eqarray}

The aligned Cartesian basis matrix is

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_{0g}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{e}_{r0}^T\\
        \m{e}_{y0}^T\\
        \m{e}_{z0}^T
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

A point is first translated to the cylindrical origin,

\begin{equation}
    \m{x}_1=\m{x}_g-\m{x}_O^c,
\end{equation}

then rotated into the aligned Cartesian system,

\begin{equation}
    \m{x}_2=\m{C}_{0g}\m{x}_1,
\end{equation}

and finally converted to cylindrical coordinate values,

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \begin{bmatrix}r\\\varphi\\z\end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \sqrt{x_2^2+y_2^2}\\
        \atantwo(y_2,x_2)\\
        z_2
    \end{bmatrix}.}
\end{equation}

The translation and rotation can also be written in homogeneous coordinates,

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}\m{x}_1\\1\end{bmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{
    \begin{bmatrix}
      1&0&0&-x_O^c\\
      0&1&0&-y_O^c\\
      0&0&1&-z_O^c\\
      0&0&0&1
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\m{T}_T}
    \begin{bmatrix}\m{x}_g\\1\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}\m{x}_2\\1\end{bmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{
    \begin{bmatrix}
      e_{r0,1}&e_{r0,2}&e_{r0,3}&0\\
      e_{y0,1}&e_{y0,2}&e_{y0,3}&0\\
      e_{z0,1}&e_{z0,2}&e_{z0,3}&0\\
      0&0&0&1
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\m{T}_R}
    \begin{bmatrix}\m{x}_1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Hence

\begin{equation}
    \m{T}=\m{T}_R\m{T}_T
\end{equation}

has the explicit form

\begin{equation}
\m{T}=
\begin{bmatrix}
 e_{r0,1}&e_{r0,2}&e_{r0,3}&-\m{e}_{r0}^T\m{x}_O^c\\
 e_{y0,1}&e_{y0,2}&e_{y0,3}&-\m{e}_{y0}^T\m{x}_O^c\\
 e_{z0,1}&e_{z0,2}&e_{z0,3}&-\m{e}_{z0}^T\m{x}_O^c\\
 0&0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Conversely, if $\m{x}_{c}=[r\cos\varphi,\;r\sin\varphi,\;z]^T$ denotes the
Cartesian representation inside the cylindrical frame, then

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \m{x}_g=\m{C}_{0g}^T\m{x}_{c}+\m{x}_O^c.}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Position-Dependent Boundary Basis}

Let $\m{x}$ be the nodal position, $\m{x}_0$ a point on the cylinder axis, and
$\m{a}$ the axis direction. First normalize the axis,

\begin{equation}
    \m{e}_z=\frac{\m{a}}{\norm{\m{a}}}.
\end{equation}

Remove the axial component from the position vector,

\begin{equation}
    \m{p}=\m{x}-\m{x}_0,
    \qquad
    \m{r}=\m{p}-\left(\m{p}^T\m{e}_z\right)\m{e}_z.
\end{equation}

The local directions are

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{e}_r
    &=&
    \frac{\m{r}}{\norm{\m{r}}},\\
    \m{e}_{\varphi}
    &=&
    \m{e}_z\times\m{e}_r,\\
    \m{C}_{cyl,g}
    &=&
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{e}_r^T\\
        \m{e}_{\varphi}^T\\
        \m{e}_z^T
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{eqarray}

A radial support is then simply

\begin{equation}
    u_r=0,
\end{equation}

while circumferential and axial motion may remain free.

\begin{python}
def cylindrical_basis(point, origin, axis):
    point = np.array(point, dtype=float)
    origin = np.array(origin, dtype=float)
    ez = normalize3(np.array(axis, dtype=float))

    p = point - origin
    axial = dot3(p, ez) * ez
    radial_vector = p - axial

    if np.sqrt(dot3(radial_vector, radial_vector)) < 1.0e-14:
        raise ValueError("radial direction undefined on cylinder axis")

    er = normalize3(radial_vector)
    ephi = normalize3(cross3(ez, er))
    return np.vstack([er, ephi, ez])


points = np.array([
    [2.0, 0.0, 1.0],
    [0.0, 2.0, 1.0],
    [-2.0, 0.0, 1.0],
])

for point in points:
    Ccyl = cylindrical_basis(point, origin=[0.0, 0.0, 0.0],
                             axis=[0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
    print("point", point)
    print(Ccyl)
\end{python}

The radial direction is undefined for a node exactly on the cylinder axis. A
solver must therefore detect this geometric singularity and either reject the
radial/tangential definition or apply an explicitly supplied local Cartesian
basis. This is an implementation issue that disappears completely in the
compact mathematical notation.

\subsection{Boundary Basis When the Cylindrical Reference Frame Is Already Known}

Suppose the fixed Cartesian frame associated with the cylindrical system has
already been constructed so that

\begin{equation}
    \m{x}_{base}=\m{C}_{0g}(\m{x}_g-\m{x}_O^c).
\end{equation}

With $\m{x}_{base}=[x\;y\;z]^T$,

\begin{eqarray}
    r&=&\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\
    \varphi&=&\atantwo(y,x),\\
    z_{cyl}&=&z.
\end{eqarray}

Since

\begin{equation}
    \cos\varphi=\frac{x}{r},
    \qquad
    \sin\varphi=\frac{y}{r},
\end{equation}

the matrix that resolves a vector from this base Cartesian frame into its
radial, circumferential and axial components is

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_{cyl,0}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x/r&y/r&0\\
        -y/r&x/r&0\\
        0&0&1
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The transpose performs the opposite vector transformation,

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_{0,cyl}
    =
    \m{C}_{cyl,0}^T
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x/r&-y/r&0\\
        y/r&\phantom{-}x/r&0\\
        0&0&1
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Thus a global displacement vector is resolved into the node-specific
cylindrical boundary basis by

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \m{u}_{cyl}
    =\m{C}_{cyl,0}\m{C}_{0g}\m{u}_g.}
\end{equation}

If the global equilibrium equation is transformed to these local coordinates,
the same congruence relation used for an ordinary skew support applies,

\begin{equation}
    \m{T}^T\m{K}\m{T}\closure{\m{u}}
    =\m{T}^T\m{f},
\end{equation}

with $\m{T}$ assembled from the appropriate local-to-global nodal basis blocks.
The cylindrical nature of the support therefore enters through construction of
the position-dependent basis, not through a different algebraic enforcement
method.

\subsection{Initial Versus Updated Local Directions}

In a geometrically linear analysis, the local basis is normally constructed
from the initial nodal coordinates and then kept fixed. A radial support at a
node therefore constrains motion along the \textit{initial} radial direction.
If large motion is permitted and the support direction is intended to follow
the current geometry, the basis must instead be updated,

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_{cyl,g}=\m{C}_{cyl,g}(\m{x}+\m{u}).
\end{equation}

The latter is a nonlinear constraint because its coefficients depend on the
unknown configuration. Whether a particular solver's ``cylindrical support''
is frozen in the initial basis or updated as a follower definition is therefore
a formulation detail that should be checked when rotations become large.

\section{Prescribed Base Motion}

A rheonomic Dirichlet condition has the form

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_p=\closure{\m{u}}_p(t).
\end{equation}

For the dynamic equilibrium equations

\begin{equation}
    \m{M}\ddot{\m{u}}
    +\m{C}\dot{\m{u}}
    +\m{K}\m{u}
    =\m{f},
\end{equation}

partitioning into free and prescribed DOFs gives the free equations

\begin{equation}\label{eq:base-excitation-partition}
\boxed{
    \m{M}_{ff}\ddot{\m{u}}_f
    +\m{C}_{ff}\dot{\m{u}}_f
    +\m{K}_{ff}\m{u}_f
    =
    \m{f}_f
    -\m{M}_{fp}\ddot{\closure{\m{u}}}_p
    -\m{C}_{fp}\dot{\closure{\m{u}}}_p
    -\m{K}_{fp}\closure{\m{u}}_p
}.
\end{equation}

Thus a prescribed base displacement, velocity and acceleration appear as
\textit{effective forcing} of the free coordinates. This is why support motion
cannot be represented merely by overwriting a displacement after a time step.
The kinematic history participates in inertia, damping and stiffness coupling.
The broader dynamics implications are developed later in the book; here the
important point is that a time-dependent essential condition is still a
Dirichlet condition, just a rheonomic one.

\section[Implementation View]{Implementation View: From Support Definition to Algebra}

A graphical finite element interface may present a support as a symbol on a
face or node, but the solver eventually needs discrete information. A typical
preprocessing path is

\begin{enumerate}
    \item identify the geometric entity and the attached nodes;
    \item determine which field components are constrained;
    \item construct any required local basis;
    \item map node/DOF identities to global equation numbers;
    \item generate prescribed values or constraint coefficients;
    \item choose the numerical enforcement route;
    \item solve the reduced or augmented equations;
    \item recover reactions and transform them to the requested output basis.
\end{enumerate}

For an axis-aligned fixed displacement, steps 3 and 5 are trivial. For a skew
roller, step 3 creates a local basis. For a cylindrical support, the basis must
be created separately at every node. For a general node-to-surface relation,
geometry additionally determines interpolation coefficients, as shown in
Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}. Contact adds repeated search,
projection and state classification in Chapter~\ref{chap:contact-formulations}.

\begin{bbox}
\textbf{Engineering note -- The support icon is not the formulation.}

Two solver features can display the same roller or cylindrical-support symbol
while constructing the algebra differently: one may rotate nodal DOFs, another
may generate explicit constraint equations, and a third may use an internal
multiplier object. For linear orthonormal kinematics these routes can represent
the same admissible motion, but they may differ in reaction recovery, nonlinear
updating, modal compatibility and solver restrictions.
\end{bbox}

\section{Common Modelling Caveats}

\subsection{Do Not Confuse Force and Displacement Boundary Data}

A force-controlled and displacement-controlled test may load the same physical
specimen, but they define different mathematical problems. Prescribing both the
force and the displacement of the same scalar boundary component generally
over-specifies the problem unless one quantity is intended only for reaction
measurement.

\subsection{A Free DOF Is Not the Same as a Zero DOF}

Leaving $u_x$ unconstrained means the solver determines $u_x$ from equilibrium.
Setting $u_x=0$ removes that admissible motion. This simple distinction is a
frequent source of unintended stiffness when symmetry, rollers or local support
directions are defined too aggressively.

\subsection{Check the Direction Basis}

A skew constraint is only as correct as its local basis. Nearly parallel input
vectors can make a three-dimensional basis ill-conditioned; cylindrical radial
directions are singular on the axis; and inconsistent axis conventions can
reverse tangential directions. Basis construction should therefore include
normalization, orthogonality and degeneracy checks rather than only formulas.

\subsection{Large Rotation Changes the Meaning of ``Direction''}

A direction fixed in the global frame, a direction fixed in the initial local
frame and a direction that follows the deforming structure are three different
constraints. They coincide only for sufficiently small rotations. This issue is
revisited for rigid and remote couplings in
Chapter~\ref{chap:kinematic-constraints}.

\subsection{Reactions Belong to the Enforced Basis}

If a support is imposed in a local normal direction, the most direct reaction
is the work-conjugate force in that local direction. Transforming reactions to
global components is straightforward, but the basis must be the same one used
for the kinematic constraint. Mixing local displacements with global reactions
can obscure equilibrium checks.

\section{Summary}

Boundary conditions are the information that turns a field equation into a
specific engineering problem. In the standard displacement formulation,
Dirichlet conditions prescribe motion, Neumann conditions prescribe force or
traction, and Robin conditions relate the two through a boundary constitutive
law such as an elastic support. These mathematical names are best understood as
compact descriptions of familiar engineering definitions rather than as
separate physical phenomena.

Kinematic constraints can simultaneously be classified as holonomic or
non-holonomic, bilateral or unilateral, scleronomic or rheonomic, and linear or
nonlinear. These classification axes answer different questions and should not
be collapsed into one vocabulary list.

At the discrete level, the simplest essential boundary condition prescribes one
DOF. A skew boundary condition remains one scalar restriction if expressed in
its natural local basis; only its global representation contains several
coefficients. Three-dimensional Cartesian and cylindrical support directions
are therefore primarily problems of constructing a reliable orthonormal basis
