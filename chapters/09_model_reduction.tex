\chapter{Model reduction}

\section{Guyan reduction}
\textbf{Guyan reduction}, also known as \textbf{static condensation},
is a \textit{dimensionality reduction} method which reduces the number
of \textbf{DOFs} by ignoring the interial terms of the equilibrium equations
and expressing the unloaded \textbf{DOFs} in terms of the loaded \textbf{DOFs}.

The static equilibrium equation:

\begin{eqarray}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{K}_{mm} & \m{K}_{ms} \\
        \m{K}_{sm} & \m{K}_{ss}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_m \\
        \m{u}_s
    \end{bmatrix} &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{f}_m \\
        \m{f}_s
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \m{K}_{mm} \m{u}_m + \m{K}_{ms} \m{u}_s &= \m{f}_m \\
    \m{K}_{sm} \m{u}_m + \m{K}_{ss} \m{u}_s &= \m{f}_s
\end{eqarray}

Where $ m $ denotes the \textbf{master} DOFs and $ s $ the \textbf{slave} DOFs.
Stating that $ s $ section of the problem DOFs is unloaded, we can
write that $ \m{f}_s = \m{0} $. Then the slave DOFs are expressed
by the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{sm} \m{u}_m + \m{K}_{ss} \m{u}_s = \m{0}
\end{equation}

Solving the above equation in terms of the independent (master) DOFs leads to
the following dependency relations:

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_s = -\m{K}_{ss}^{-1} \m{K}_{sm} \m{u}_m
\end{equation}

Substituting the dependency relations to the upper (master) partition of the static
equilibrium problem condenses away the slave DOFs, leading to the following reduced
system of linear equations:

\begin{equation}
    \left(\m{K}_{mm} - \m{K}_{ms} \m{K}_{ss}^{-1} \m{K}_{sm} \right)
    \m{u}_m = \m{f}_m
\end{equation}

The above system of linear equations is equivalent to the original problem, but expressed
in terms of \textbf{master} DOFs alone. Thus, the \textbf{Guyan} reduction method
results in a reduced system by condensing away the \textbf{slave} DOFs.

The \textbf{Guyan reduction} can be also expressed as a \textit{change of basis} which
produces a low-dimensional representation of the original space, represented by the
\textbf{master} DOFs. The linear transformation that maps the reduced space onto
the full space is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_m \\
        \m{u}_s
    \end{bmatrix} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{I} \\
        -\m{K}_{ss}^{-1} \m{K}_{sm}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \m{u}_m = \m{T}_G \m{u}_m
\end{equation}

where $ \m{T}_G $ represents the \textbf{Guyan} reduction \textit{transformation matrix}.
Thus, the reduced problem is represented as:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_G \m{u}_m = \m{f}_m
\end{equation}

In the above equation, $ \m{K}_G $ represents the reduced system of linear equations
that's obtained by applying the \textbf{Guyan reduction} transformation on the
full system, which is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_G = \m{T}_G^T \m{K} \m{T}_G
\end{equation}

where $ \m{T}_G $ has the dimension of $ n_{DOF} \times n_m $.


\section{Craig-Bampton reduction}

The \textbf{Craig-Bampton reduction}, also known as the \textbf{fixed-interface
component mode synthesis}, extends the \textbf{Guyan reduction} by supplementing
the static deformation shapes with a truncated set of dynamic deformation shapes.
It therefore retains the exact static relation between the interface and internal
DOFs, while approximating the internal dynamic deformation by a modal base.

The dynamic equilibrium equation is partitioned into the \textbf{boundary} DOFs
$ b $ and the \textbf{internal} DOFs $ i $:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{M}_{bb} & \m{M}_{bi} \\
        \m{M}_{ib} & \m{M}_{ii}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \ddot{\m{u}}_b \\
        \ddot{\m{u}}_i
    \end{bmatrix}
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{K}_{bb} & \m{K}_{bi} \\
        \m{K}_{ib} & \m{K}_{ii}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_b \\
        \m{u}_i
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{f}_b \\
        \m{f}_i
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where the boundary DOFs are retained in physical coordinates and the internal DOFs
are reduced. In the terminology used for the \textbf{Guyan reduction}, the boundary
DOFs correspond to the master DOFs and the internal DOFs to the slave DOFs.

\subsection{Constraint modes}

The \textbf{constraint modes}, also called the \textbf{static constraint modes},
describe the static deformation of the internal DOFs caused by a unit displacement
of each individual boundary DOF. The inertia terms and the externally applied
forces on the internal DOFs are set to zero:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{ib} \m{u}_b + \m{K}_{ii} \m{u}_i = \m{0}
\end{equation}

The internal displacement is then expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_i = -\m{K}_{ii}^{-1}\m{K}_{ib}\m{u}_b
             = \M{\Psi}_c \m{u}_b
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{equation}
    \M{\Psi}_c = -\m{K}_{ii}^{-1}\m{K}_{ib}
\end{equation}

is the \textbf{constraint mode matrix}. Each column of $ \M{\Psi}_c $ is the
internal static displacement field corresponding to a unit displacement of one
boundary DOF, while all remaining boundary DOFs are held fixed.

The inverse $ \m{K}_{ii}^{-1} $ is only a mathematical notation. Numerically, the
constraint modes are obtained by solving the multiple right-hand-side problem:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{ii}\M{\Psi}_c = -\m{K}_{ib}
\end{equation}

The matrix $ \m{K}_{ii} $ is factorised only once and the factorisation is reused
for all boundary DOFs.

\subsection{Fixed-interface normal modes}

The dynamic deformation that cannot be represented by the constraint modes is
approximated using the \textbf{fixed-interface normal modes}. These modes are
calculated by fixing all boundary DOFs:

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_b = \m{0}
\end{equation}

and solving the internal generalised eigenvalue problem:

\begin{equation}
    \left( \m{K}_{ii} - \omega_j^2 \m{M}_{ii} \right)
    \M{\varphi}_j = \m{0}
\end{equation}

The retained fixed-interface modes are assembled column-wise into the matrix:

\begin{equation}
    \M{\Phi}_i =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \M{\varphi}_1 & \M{\varphi}_2 & \dots & \M{\varphi}_{n_\varphi}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $ n_\varphi $ is the number of retained fixed-interface modes. When the modes
are mass-normalised:

\begin{eqarray}
    \M{\Phi}_i^T \m{M}_{ii} \M{\Phi}_i &= \m{I} \\
    \M{\Phi}_i^T \m{K}_{ii} \M{\Phi}_i &= \M{\Omega}_i^2
\end{eqarray}

where:

\begin{equation}
    \M{\Omega}_i^2 =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \omega_1^2 & \dots & 0 \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0 & \dots & \omega_{n_\varphi}^2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

is the diagonal matrix of the retained fixed-interface eigenvalues.

\subsection{Craig-Bampton transformation}

The internal displacement is represented as a superposition of the static
constraint modes and the fixed-interface normal modes:

\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_i = \M{\Psi}_c \m{u}_b + \M{\Phi}_i \M{\eta}
\end{equation}

where $ \M{\eta} $ is the vector of the fixed-interface modal coordinates. The full
physical displacement vector is therefore expressed as:

\begin{eqarray}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_b \\
        \m{u}_i
    \end{bmatrix}
    &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{I} & \m{0} \\
        \M{\Psi}_c & \M{\Phi}_i
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_b \\
        \M{\eta}
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \m{u} &= \m{T}_{CB}\m{q}_{CB}
\end{eqarray}

where:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{T}_{CB} &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{I} & \m{0} \\
        \M{\Psi}_c & \M{\Phi}_i
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \m{q}_{CB} &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{u}_b \\
        \M{\eta}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{eqarray}

The Craig-Bampton coordinates therefore consist of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the physical displacements of all retained boundary DOFs,
    \item the modal amplitudes of the retained fixed-interface normal modes.
\end{itemize}

Substituting $ \m{u} = \m{T}_{CB}\m{q}_{CB} $ to the full equation of motion and
premultiplying it with $ \m{T}_{CB}^T $ gives the reduced equation:

\begin{equation}
    \m{M}_{CB}\ddot{\m{q}}_{CB}
    + \m{C}_{CB}\dot{\m{q}}_{CB}
    + \m{K}_{CB}\m{q}_{CB}
    = \m{f}_{CB}
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{M}_{CB} &= \m{T}_{CB}^T \m{M} \m{T}_{CB} \\
    \m{C}_{CB} &= \m{T}_{CB}^T \m{C} \m{T}_{CB} \\
    \m{K}_{CB} &= \m{T}_{CB}^T \m{K} \m{T}_{CB} \\
    \m{f}_{CB} &= \m{T}_{CB}^T \m{f}
\end{eqarray}

For exact constraint modes and a symmetric stiffness matrix, the reduced
stiffness matrix has the following block-diagonal form:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{CB} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{K}_{G} & \m{0} \\
        \m{0} & \M{\Omega}_i^2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{G} = \m{K}_{bb}
    - \m{K}_{bi}\m{K}_{ii}^{-1}\m{K}_{ib}
\end{equation}

is the \textbf{Guyan-condensed stiffness matrix}. The corresponding mass matrix is
generally not block-diagonal:

\begin{equation}
    \m{M}_{CB} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \m{M}_{bb}^{CB} & \m{M}_{b\varphi}^{CB} \\
        \m{M}_{\varphi b}^{CB} & \m{I}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

with:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{M}_{bb}^{CB} &= \m{M}_{bb}
    + \m{M}_{bi}\M{\Psi}_c
    + \M{\Psi}_c^T\m{M}_{ib}
    + \M{\Psi}_c^T\m{M}_{ii}\M{\Psi}_c \\
    \m{M}_{b\varphi}^{CB} &=
    \left( \m{M}_{bi} + \M{\Psi}_c^T\m{M}_{ii} \right)\M{\Phi}_i \\
    \m{M}_{\varphi b}^{CB} &= \left( \m{M}_{b\varphi}^{CB} \right)^T
\end{eqarray}

The coupling in the reduced mass matrix is the reason why the Craig-Bampton
coordinates are not normal modes of the unconstrained component. The boundary
coordinates remain physical and are dynamically coupled with the fixed-interface
modal coordinates.

\begin{bbox}[0.96]
    \textbf{Note}:

    The Craig-Bampton transformation does not reduce the boundary DOFs. If a
    component has $ n_b $ boundary DOFs and $ n_\varphi $ retained fixed-interface
    modes, then the reduced model has:

    \begin{equation}
        n_{CB} = n_b + n_\varphi
    \end{equation}

    generalised coordinates. A large number of interface nodes can therefore
    still result in a relatively large Craig-Bampton model.
\end{bbox}


\section{Flexible body modal reduction}

A \textbf{flexible body} used in a multibody or reduced-order simulation is often
represented by a small set of free-interface normal modes instead of keeping all
Craig-Bampton boundary coordinates as independent generalised coordinates. This
can be obtained by performing a second modal reduction in the Craig-Bampton space.

The Craig-Bampton equation without damping and external forces is:

\begin{equation}
    \m{M}_{CB}\ddot{\m{q}}_{CB}
    + \m{K}_{CB}\m{q}_{CB} = \m{0}
\end{equation}

No boundary coordinates are constrained in the second reduction pass. The
free-interface generalised eigenvalue problem is therefore:

\begin{equation}
    \left( \m{K}_{CB} - \omega_j^2 \m{M}_{CB} \right)
    \M{\xi}_j = \m{0}
\end{equation}

The eigenvectors are assembled into the reduced free-interface mode matrix:

\begin{equation}
    \M{\Xi} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \M{\xi}_1 & \M{\xi}_2 & \dots & \M{\xi}_{n_f}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $ n_f $ is the number of retained free-interface modes. The Craig-Bampton
coordinates are then represented by:

\begin{equation}
    \m{q}_{CB} = \M{\Xi}\m{q}_f
\end{equation}

and the full physical displacement is recovered as:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{u} &= \m{T}_{CB}\m{q}_{CB} \\
           &= \m{T}_{CB}\M{\Xi}\m{q}_f \\
           &= \m{T}_f\m{q}_f
\end{eqarray}

where:

\begin{equation}
    \m{T}_f = \m{T}_{CB}\M{\Xi}
\end{equation}

is the final \textbf{flexible body transformation matrix}. The columns of
$ \m{T}_f $ are free-interface mode shapes expressed at the original FE DOFs.
The interface displacement is no longer an independent coordinate. It is
approximated by the retained flexible body modes in the same way as the internal
displacement.

The final reduced matrices are calculated as:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{M}_f &= \M{\Xi}^T \m{M}_{CB}\M{\Xi}
             = \m{T}_f^T\m{M}\m{T}_f \\
    \m{K}_f &= \M{\Xi}^T \m{K}_{CB}\M{\Xi}
             = \m{T}_f^T\m{K}\m{T}_f \\
    \m{C}_f &= \M{\Xi}^T \m{C}_{CB}\M{\Xi}
             = \m{T}_f^T\m{C}\m{T}_f \\
    \m{f}_f &= \M{\Xi}^T\m{f}_{CB}
             = \m{T}_f^T\m{f}
\end{eqarray}

When the free-interface eigenvectors are mass-normalised:

\begin{eqarray}
    \M{\Xi}^T \m{M}_{CB}\M{\Xi} &= \m{I} \\
    \M{\Xi}^T \m{K}_{CB}\M{\Xi} &= \M{\Omega}_f^2
\end{eqarray}

The flexible body equation of motion then becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \ddot{\m{q}}_f + \m{C}_f\dot{\m{q}}_f
    + \M{\Omega}_f^2\m{q}_f = \m{f}_f
\end{equation}

For classical modal damping, the damping matrix can be represented by a diagonal
matrix:

\begin{equation}
    \m{C}_f =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        2\zeta_1\omega_1 & \dots & 0 \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0 & \dots & 2\zeta_{n_f}\omega_{n_f}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Rigid body modes}

A free three-dimensional component has six \textbf{rigid body modes}: three
translations and three rotations. Their eigenvalues are theoretically equal to
zero because a rigid body displacement produces no elastic strain energy:

\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{CB}\M{\xi}_r = \m{0}
\end{equation}

In finite-precision arithmetic, the rigid body eigenvalues are usually small but
not exactly zero. They must therefore be identified using a tolerance relative to
the characteristic stiffness or to the first elastic eigenvalue, rather than by
checking whether the calculated frequency is exactly zero.

The exact treatment of the rigid body modes depends on the formulation of the
surrounding simulation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item when the flexible component is described only by modal coordinates, the
          rigid body modes must be retained to allow free translation and rotation,
    \item when the multibody formulation describes the rigid body position and
          orientation separately, the six rigid body modes are not used as elastic
          coordinates; the retained elastic modes must nevertheless remain
          mass-orthogonal to the rigid body motion.
\end{itemize}

The second case is commonly used by floating-frame flexible body formulations. The
large rigid motion is described by the reference frame of the body and the modal
coordinates describe only the small elastic deformation relative to this frame.

\subsection{Mode truncation}

If all $ n_{CB} $ eigenvectors of the reduced free-interface eigenproblem are kept,
the transformation $ \M{\Xi} $ represents only another basis of the same
Craig-Bampton space. The second reduction becomes a dimensionality reduction only
when the mode matrix is truncated:

\begin{equation}
    n_f < n_{CB}
\end{equation}

This provides the distinction between the two reduced representations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the Craig-Bampton model retains all boundary DOFs and therefore preserves
          an independent physical coordinate at every interface DOF,
    \item the flexible body modal model retains only selected free-interface modes
          and therefore approximates both the boundary and internal deformation by
          a smaller number of modal coordinates.
\end{itemize}

The accuracy of the final flexible body base is limited by both truncation stages.
The fixed-interface base must first be rich enough to represent the required
internal dynamics. The free-interface base is then selected according to the
frequency range and deformation patterns required by the final simulation.
Increasing the number of free-interface modes cannot recover deformation that was
already excluded from the Craig-Bampton base.

\subsection{Numerical procedure}

A numerically efficient implementation of the complete reduction can be summarised
as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reorder the FE matrices into boundary and internal DOFs.
    \item Factorise $ \m{K}_{ii} $ and solve
          $ \m{K}_{ii}\M{\Psi}_c = -\m{K}_{ib} $ for all constraint modes.
    \item Solve the lowest required eigenpairs of
          $ \m{K}_{ii}\M{\varphi} = \omega^2\m{M}_{ii}\M{\varphi} $
          with the boundary fixed.
    \item Mass-normalise the fixed-interface modes and assemble $ \m{T}_{CB} $.
    \item Calculate $ \m{M}_{CB} $, $ \m{K}_{CB} $ and, when required,
          $ \m{C}_{CB} $ by congruent transformation.
    \item Solve the free-interface eigenproblem
          $ \m{K}_{CB}\M{\xi} = \omega^2\m{M}_{CB}\M{\xi} $.
    \item Identify the rigid body modes and select the required elastic modes.
    \item Mass-normalise the retained eigenvectors and assemble
          $ \m{T}_f = \m{T}_{CB}\M{\Xi} $.
    \item Calculate the final modal matrices and the load and output projection
          operators.
\end{enumerate}

The following numerical considerations are important:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The matrix inverse must not be formed explicitly. A sparse factorisation
          and multiple right-hand-side solve should be used for the constraint modes.
    \item The fixed-interface stiffness matrix $ \m{K}_{ii} $ must be nonsingular.
          A singular matrix indicates an internal rigid body motion or a mechanism
          that is not restrained by the selected boundary DOFs.
    \item Symmetric generalised eigensolvers preserve the mass orthogonality of
          distinct modes. An ordinary Euclidean Gram-Schmidt process is therefore
          not normally applied to the eigenvectors.
    \item Repeated or tightly clustered eigenvalues can require
          re-orthogonalisation of the corresponding mode block. This must use the
          mass inner product $ \M{\xi}_r^T\m{M}_{CB}\M{\xi}_s $.
    \item The Craig-Bampton matrices should be symmetrised only to remove small
          round-off asymmetry, not to hide an incorrectly assembled transformation.
    \item Mode selection should be based on the required frequency range and on the
          deformation and loading patterns, rather than on the mode count alone.
\end{itemize}

For a retained mode $ \M{\xi}_j $, the mass normalisation is performed as:

\begin{equation}
    \M{\xi}_{j,mass} =
    \frac{\M{\xi}_j}
    {\sqrt{\M{\xi}_j^T\m{M}_{CB}\M{\xi}_j}}
\end{equation}

The orthogonality and the eigenpair residual can be checked using:

\begin{eqarray}
    \m{E}_M &= \M{\Xi}^T\m{M}_{CB}\M{\Xi} - \m{I} \\
    \m{E}_K &= \M{\Xi}^T\m{K}_{CB}\M{\Xi} - \M{\Omega}_f^2 \\
    \m{r}_j &= \m{K}_{CB}\M{\xi}_j
              - \omega_j^2\m{M}_{CB}\M{\xi}_j
\end{eqarray}

A valid reduced base should have small off-diagonal entries in $ \m{E}_M $ and
$ \m{E}_K $, and a small residual norm $ \norm{\m{r}_j} $ relative to the norms
of the stiffness and inertia terms.

\begin{bbox}[0.96]
    \textbf{Note}:

    The free-interface modes calculated in the Craig-Bampton space are not the
    original full FE free-free modes. They are approximations restricted to the
    subspace spanned by $ \m{T}_{CB} $. After mapping with
    $ \m{T}_f = \m{T}_{CB}\M{\Xi} $, they have the same physical meaning and
    converge towards the full FE free-free modes as the Craig-Bampton base is
    enriched.
\end{bbox}


\begin{bbox}[0.96]
    \textbf{Scope of the classical formulation}:

    The above derivation assumes a linear time-invariant system with symmetric
    mass and stiffness matrices. For a component linearised about a prestressed
    operating point, the tangent stiffness matrix can be used in the same
    reduction procedure.

    When a velocity-dependent gyroscopic matrix $ \m{G} $ is retained, it must be
    transformed together with the other system matrices:

    \begin{equation}
        \m{G}_{CB} = \m{T}_{CB}^T\m{G}\m{T}_{CB}
    \end{equation}

    The free-interface problem then contains the term $ i\omega\m{G}_{CB} $ and is
    no longer the symmetric real-valued eigenproblem described above. Its modes
    can be complex and the simple mass-orthogonality relations do not generally
    apply.
\end{bbox}
