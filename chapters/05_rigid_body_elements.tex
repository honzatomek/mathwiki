\chapter{Rigid Elements and Remote Couplings}
\label{chap:rigid-elements}

\section{From Multifreedom Constraints to Rigid Elements}

Finite element meshes describe a structure by means of ordinary structural
finite elements whose nodes carry a finite set of degrees of freedom. Practical
models, however, also require relationships between degrees of freedom that do
not correspond to the constitutive behaviour of a beam, shell or solid. A load
may have to be introduced at a point outside the mesh, a bearing reaction may
have to be distributed over a housing, or a group of nodes may have to move as
if it belonged to a perfectly rigid flange. Such relations are represented by
\textit{multifreedom constraints} rather than by adding an arbitrarily stiff
structural part.

Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints} introduced the general constraint
relation and the principal methods used to impose it. In matrix form, a set of
linear constraints may be written as
\begin{equation}
    \m{A}\m{u}=\m{g},
    \label{rbe-general-mfc}
\end{equation}
where $\m{u}$ is the global displacement vector, $\m{A}$ contains the
constraint coefficients, and $\m{g}$ contains prescribed offsets. A rigid
element is best understood as a convenient generator of selected rows of
$\m{A}$. The solver may subsequently impose those rows by elimination,
Lagrange multipliers, a penalty formulation, or another method discussed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}.

This interpretation is important. A classical rigid element is not necessarily
a material-bearing finite element. In its ideal constraint form it has no
Young's modulus, no cross-sectional area and no strain energy of its own. Its
purpose is to prescribe how selected degrees of freedom are related. Some
programs additionally provide massive rigid bodies, rigid surfaces or penalty
couplings; these are related modelling tools, but they should not be confused
with an exact multipoint constraint.

The constraints in this chapter are normally \textit{bilateral}: once present,
they act in both directions and remain active. This distinguishes them from the
unilateral contact conditions of Chapter~\ref{chap:contact-formulations}, for
which the active set is part of the solution. An RBE cannot open, close, slide
or change from stick to slip. If such behaviour is physically possible, contact
or a dedicated connector formulation is required.

\begin{bbox}[0.94]
    \textbf{Central idea.} RBE1, RBE2, RBE3 and modern remote couplings do not
    represent four unrelated technologies. They are different ways of defining
    kinematic relations and of transferring their energetically conjugate
    forces. Their differences lie mainly in which degrees of freedom are
    independent, how the interpolation is constructed, and whether the
    connected region is forced to remain rigid.
\end{bbox}

\section{Historical Development of the Rigid-Element Family}

Early finite element programs allowed analysts to enter multipoint equations
directly. This was general, but inconvenient: even a simple rigid spider in
three dimensions requires several coefficients for every connected node. The
rigid-element family developed as a compact input language for recurring
constraint patterns. The terminology RBE1, RBE2 and RBE3 is strongly associated
with NASTRAN and has since become common engineering vocabulary, even where
another solver uses different element names.

The progression can be read as a sequence of modelling needs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RBE1} groups selected degrees of freedom into independent and
        dependent sets and enforces general linear compatibility. It is close
        to an explicit multipoint-constraint definition.
    \item \textbf{RBE2} generates the equations of rigid-body motion from one
        independent reference node to any number of dependent nodes. It is the
        natural choice when the connected region is intended to be perfectly
        rigid.
    \item \textbf{RBE3} was introduced for interpolation and load distribution
        without turning the connected mesh patch into a rigid body. The
        reference motion is inferred from a weighted group of surrounding
        degrees of freedom.
    \item \textbf{Remote points and coupling objects} provide a higher-level
        graphical interface. According to the selected behaviour, the program
        creates a pilot node, constraint equations, contact-like coupling
        elements, or solver-specific interpolation operators.
\end{enumerate}

The numbering must not be interpreted as a sequence of increasingly accurate
elements. RBE2 and RBE3 solve different physical modelling problems. Replacing
one with the other changes the admissible deformation of the connected region.

\section{General Kinematic and Static Framework}

Let the retained or independent degrees of freedom be collected in
$\m{q}$ and the complete displacement vector in $\m{u}$. A linear coupling
may be represented by
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}=\m{T}\m{q}+\m{u}_0,
    \label{rbe-transformation-general}
\end{equation}
where $\m{T}$ is a transformation or interpolation matrix and $\m{u}_0$ is a
prescribed offset. For a homogeneous rigid coupling, $\m{u}_0=\m{0}$.
Substitution into the virtual work of the unconstrained finite element model,
\begin{equation}
    \delta\m{u}^{T}(\m{K}\m{u}-\m{f})=0,
\end{equation}
gives
\begin{equation}
    \delta\m{q}^{T}\m{T}^{T}(\m{K}\m{T}\m{q}-\m{f})=0.
\end{equation}
Consequently, an elimination implementation produces
\begin{equation}
    \m{K}_{r}=\m{T}^{T}\m{K}\m{T},
    \qquad
    \m{f}_{r}=\m{T}^{T}\m{f}.
    \label{rbe-reduced-system}
\end{equation}
This is the same transformation structure used in the master--slave method of
Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}. It also explains the reciprocal
relationship between displacement interpolation and force transfer. If
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_{r}=\m{H}\m{u}_{c},
\end{equation}
then preservation of virtual work requires
\begin{equation}
    \m{f}_{c}=\m{H}^{T}\m{f}_{r}.
    \label{rbe-virtual-work-force}
\end{equation}
The transpose is not an arbitrary load-sharing rule: it is required for energy
consistency.

A useful distinction is therefore:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an \textit{exact kinematic coupling}, which restricts the admissible
        deformation space; and
    \item an \textit{interpolation coupling}, which evaluates a reference motion
        from surrounding motion and transfers the conjugate load back through
        the transpose operator.
\end{itemize}
RBE2 is the classical example of the first class and RBE3 of the second.

\section{RBE1: General Linear Rigid Coupling}

RBE1 is the most general member of the traditional rigid-element family. It
specifies a set of independent degrees of freedom and a set of dependent degrees
of freedom. The dependent motions are represented as linear combinations of the
independent motions. In compact form,
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_{d}=\m{C}\m{u}_{i},
    \label{rbe1-relation}
\end{equation}
where the selected components of $\m{u}_{i}$ and $\m{u}_{d}$ need not be all
six degrees of freedom at their respective nodes.

RBE1 is useful when the desired coupling cannot be described by one reference
node and a standard rigid-body transformation. It can, for example, relate
selected translations to a collection of independent translations and
rotations while leaving other components free. Its generality is also its main
disadvantage: the user must understand the coefficient set and avoid redundant
or contradictory constraints. Modern graphical preprocessors therefore more
often expose direct MPC equations or named coupling objects than an explicit
RBE1 card.

\begin{bbox}[0.94]
    \textbf{Engineering note -- dependent degrees of freedom.} A degree of
    freedom that is dependent in one exact MPC should not simultaneously be
    prescribed by an incompatible single-point constraint or made dependent by
    another overlapping spider. Such definitions can create redundant rows in
    the constraint matrix, solver pivots close to zero, or a physically locked
    model.
\end{bbox}

\section{RBE2: Rigid Kinematic Coupling}

\begin{bbox}[0.95]
    For infinitesimal motion, the rigid-body relations between a dependent (slave) node and the independent (master or reference) node are:
    \begin{eqarray}
        \m{T}_s &= \m{T}_m + \m{R}_s \times \overline{\m{x}} \\
        \m{R}_s &= \m{R}_m
    \end{eqarray}

    where $ \overline{\m{x}} $ is the initial position vector from the reference node to the dependent node:
    \begin{equation}
        \overline{\m{x}} =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \overline{x} \\
            \overline{y} \\
            \overline{z}
        \end{bmatrix} =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            x_s - x_m \\
            y_s - y_m \\
            z_s - z_m
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}

    Then \textit{vector multiplication} (cross product) is defined as:
    \begin{eqarray}
        \m{R}_s \times \m{x}_s
        &= \m{R}_m \times \m{x} = & \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}
             \varphi_m \\
             \psi_m \\
             \theta_m
           \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix}
             x_s - x_m \\
             y_s - y_m \\
             z_s - z_m
           \end{bmatrix}
        &= \begin{bmatrix}
             \varphi_m \\
             \psi_m \\
             \theta_m
           \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix}
             \overline{x} \\
             \overline{y} \\
             \overline{z}
           \end{bmatrix} = \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}
             \psi_m (z_s - z_m ) - \theta_m (y_s - y_m) \\
             \theta_m (x_s - x_m) - \varphi_m (z_s - z_m) \\
             \varphi_m (y_s - y_m) - \psi_m (x_s - x_m)
           \end{bmatrix}
        &= \begin{bmatrix}
            \psi_m \overline{z} - \theta_m \overline{y} \\
            \theta_m \overline{x} - \varphi_m \overline{z} \\
            \varphi_m \overline{y} - \psi_m \overline{x}
        \end{bmatrix} = \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}
            \phantom{-}0 & \phantom{-}\overline{z} & -\overline{y} \\
            -\overline{z} & \phantom{-}0 & \phantom{-}\overline{x} \\
            \phantom{-}\overline{y} & -\overline{x} & \phantom{-}0
        \end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \varphi_m \\
            \psi_m \\
            \theta_m
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{eqarray}
\end{bbox}

\begin{bbox}[0.95]

        \end{bmatrix}

\section{RBE3: Interpolation Without a Rigid Patch}

\subsection{Motivation}

An RBE2 is appropriate only when the connected region is physically rigid or
when its deformation is intentionally neglected. If an RBE2 reference node is
connected to a thin flange, all dependent nodes are forced to follow one rigid
motion. Local bending and warping of the flange are suppressed, its apparent
stiffness increases, and natural frequencies and stresses may change.

RBE3 addresses a different problem. It defines the motion of a reference node
from a weighted set of surrounding degrees of freedom, but it does not require
those surrounding nodes to move as a rigid body. A load applied at the reference
node is distributed back to the surrounding nodes through the transpose of the
interpolation. The surrounding mesh remains free to deform according to its own
stiffness.

In the ideal MPC interpretation, the RBE3 adds no strain energy and therefore no
material stiffness. This does not mean that a model containing an RBE3 has no
load path: the load path is completed by the structural elements attached to the
interpolation nodes and by any spring, bushing or boundary condition attached to
the reference node.

\subsection{Weighted Translational Interpolation}

For a scalar translational component, the simplest interpolation is a weighted
average,
\begin{equation}
    u_r=\sum_{i=1}^{n} h_i u_i,
    \qquad
    h_i=\frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_j},
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}h_i=1.
    \label{rbe3-scalar-interpolation}
\end{equation}
The normalization ensures that a uniform translation of all connected nodes is
reproduced exactly at the reference node. In vector form,
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_r=\m{H}\m{u}_c,
    \label{rbe3-interpolation}
\end{equation}
where $\m{u}_c$ collects the selected connected-node degrees of freedom. A
three-dimensional RBE3 also requires sufficient geometric information to
reproduce rotations and moments. Practical implementations construct
$\m{H}$ from the selected components, their weights and their positions
relative to a weighted centroid.

The weighted centroid is
\begin{equation}
    \m{x}_{w}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i\m{x}_i}
                         {\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i},
    \qquad
    \m{r}_i=\m{x}_i-\m{x}_{w}.
    \label{rbe3-weighted-centroid}
\end{equation}
It is a convenient origin for separating average translation from rotational
motion. To reproduce a general rigid-body motion, the connected-node field is
approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_i \approx \m{u}_{w}+\M{\theta}_{w}\times\m{r}_i.
    \label{rbe3-rigid-fit}
\end{equation}
The reference translation and rotation may be obtained from a weighted
least-squares fit of this field. Solver formulations differ in how selected
rotational degrees of freedom, component weights, singular geometries and
additional dependent components are handled. Equation
\eqref{rbe3-rigid-fit} expresses the common principle rather than a universal
input-card algorithm.

\subsection{Force Distribution from Virtual Work}

Let a generalized reference load $\m{f}_r$ act at the RBE3 reference node. From
Equation~\eqref{rbe3-interpolation},
\begin{equation}
    \delta W
    =\m{f}_r^{T}\delta\m{u}_r
    =\m{f}_r^{T}\m{H}\delta\m{u}_c
    =(\m{H}^{T}\m{f}_r)^{T}\delta\m{u}_c.
\end{equation}
Thus the statically and energetically consistent connected-node load is
\begin{equation}
    \m{f}_c=\m{H}^{T}\m{f}_r.
    \label{rbe3-load-distribution}
\end{equation}
The distribution depends on geometry, selected components and weights, not on
the stiffness of the attached structure. The subsequent deformation and stress
field do depend on structural stiffness.

For a pure force and equal scalar translation weights, the direct force part is
shared equally. A reference moment requires a self-equilibrated pattern of
forces whose resultant is zero and whose moment equals the applied moment. In a
planar example with nodes at $\m{r}_i=[x_i,y_i,0]^T$, one convenient minimum
weighted-norm distribution for a moment $M_z$ has the form
\begin{equation}
    \m{p}_i=
    \frac{w_i M_z}{\sum_j w_j(x_j^2+y_j^2)}
    \begin{bmatrix}-y_i \\ x_i \\ 0\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{rbe3-planar-moment-distribution}
\end{equation}
It satisfies $\sum_i\m{p}_i=\m{0}$ when the coordinates are taken about the
weighted centroid and produces $\sum_i(\m{r}_i\times\m{p}_i)_z=M_z$.

\subsection{Worked Square-Patch Example}

Consider four connected nodes at the corners of a square,
\begin{equation}
    (x_i,y_i)\in\{(-a,-a),(a,-a),(a,a),(-a,a)\},
\end{equation}
with equal weights and a reference node at the centre. A force
$F_z=4\,\mathrm{kN}$ applied at the reference node is distributed as
\begin{equation}
    F_{z,i}=1\,\mathrm{kN}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,4.
\end{equation}
This distribution has the correct total force and no resultant moment.

For a pure moment $M_z$, Equation~\eqref{rbe3-planar-moment-distribution}
gives tangential corner forces. Since $x_i^2+y_i^2=2a^2$ and there are four
nodes,
\begin{equation}
    \m{p}_i=\frac{M_z}{8a^2}
    \begin{bmatrix}-y_i \\ x_i \\ 0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Their vector sum is zero, while their combined moment is $M_z$. The square may
still bend or warp because no equation requires the four corners to remain in a
rigid plane.

\subsection{RBE3 Reference Node as a Deformable Support}

A particularly useful application is a support whose reaction must be spread
over a flexible surface. Let an RBE3 reference node be coupled to a patch of
shell or solid nodes. Connect the reference node to ground, or to another body,
by translational and rotational springs, a bushing, or a general connector. The
reference displacement is interpolated from the surface motion, and the spring
reaction is distributed back through Equation~\eqref{rbe3-load-distribution}.

The compliance then comes from two sources:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the explicitly defined spring or bushing stiffness; and
    \item the natural deformation of the coupled surface.
\end{enumerate}
The RBE3 itself contributes neither. This makes the construction suitable for
rubber mounts, bearing supports, resilient machine feet and simplified joint
models. An RBE2 in the same position would additionally rigidize the surface
patch and could substantially overestimate support stiffness.

A common implementation uses coincident nodes. One node belongs to the
structural mesh; a second coincident node is the RBE3 reference or connector
node. Springs between coincident nodes may represent translational or rotational
compliance without introducing an artificial geometric lever arm. Coincidence
alone does not merge the nodes: their relative motion is controlled only by the
defined springs and constraints.

\begin{bbox}[0.94]
    \textbf{Engineering note -- the support is deformable, not the RBE3.}
    Calling this arrangement a ``deformable RBE3 support'' is convenient but
    imprecise. The RBE3 supplies interpolation and load transfer. Deformation
    occurs in the connected structure and in the spring or bushing.
\end{bbox}

\subsection{RBE2 and RBE3 Compared}

\begin{tabular}{p{0.25\textwidth}|p{0.31\textwidth}|p{0.31\textwidth}}
    & \textbf{RBE2} & \textbf{RBE3} \\ \hline

\section{Remote Points and Remote Couplings}

\subsection{Remote Point as a Modelling Object}

A remote point in a graphical preprocessor is usually not a new physical finite
element. It is a modelling object that defines a location, an orientation, a
scope of mesh entities and a coupling behaviour. During solver input generation,
the program creates a pilot node and one or more constraint, contact or
interpolation entities.

In ANSYS Mechanical, remote boundary conditions and remote loads are associated
with a remote point and a selected behaviour such as rigid or deformable. The
precise generated entities depend on the object, scoped topology, analysis type
and solver formulation. Therefore the convenient rules
``rigid remote point equals RBE2'' and ``deformable remote point equals RBE3''
are useful first approximations, but not universal identities.

\subsection{What the Remote Definition Solves}

A remote definition automates several otherwise manual tasks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item creation of a reference or pilot node outside the mesh;
    \item transformation between the remote coordinate system and nodal bases;
    \item generation of rigid or deformable coupling equations;
    \item transfer of forces and moments between the remote location and a
        distributed mesh region;
    \item application of remote displacement components and connector elements;
    \item recovery and presentation of reactions at a convenient engineering
        location.
\end{itemize}

A \textit{remote force} solves the practical problem of applying a force at a
point where no structural node exists while preserving the corresponding
resultant and moment. A \textit{remote displacement} prescribes selected
components at the pilot node and transfers the resulting kinematic restrictions
to the scoped surface. A \textit{remote moment} generates an equivalent force
pattern over the surface. A spring attached to a remote point creates a compact
representation of a distributed support.

\subsection{Rigid and Deformable Behaviour}

Rigid behaviour forces the scoped nodes to follow the reference motion as a
rigid body. It is appropriate where the omitted component is much stiffer than
the retained structure, for example a thick rigid insert. Deformable behaviour
allows the scoped surface to bend and warp while constructing the remote motion
from its distributed displacement field. It is preferable for load introduction
or support coupling when surface flexibility matters.

The remote point itself does not establish physical correctness. The analyst
must still decide which surface is scoped, which components are coupled, which
coordinate system is used, and whether a rigid or deformable relation matches
the omitted hardware.

\section{Advanced Topics and Solver Caveats}

\subsection{Thermal Expansion of Rigid Couplings}

A geometrically rigid coupling is commonly formulated from the initial nodal
separations. In a uniform temperature change, the surrounding material would
freely expand according to
\begin{equation}
    \Delta L=\alpha L\Delta T.
\end{equation}
An ordinary rigid spider that preserves its original dimensions can suppress
this expansion and generate artificial thermal reactions and stresses.

Some solver implementations therefore allow a coefficient of thermal expansion
or an equivalent thermal strain for rigid elements and coupling entities. The
constraint then follows a thermally expanded reference geometry, conceptually
\begin{equation}
    L_T=L_0(1+\alpha_{r}\Delta T).
\end{equation}
Selecting $\alpha_r$ consistently with the represented material allows the
rigid connection to remain rigid relative to its thermally expanding reference
shape. This capability is solver-specific and must be verified before relying
on it. Where it is unavailable, a deformable coupling, explicit stiff component,
or carefully designed constraint topology may be required.

\subsection{Small- and Large-Displacement Formulations}

The familiar RBE2 relation
\begin{equation}
    \m{u}_s=\m{u}_m+\M{\theta}_m\times\m{r}_0
    \label{rbe-small-rotation}
\end{equation}
is a linearization about the initial geometry. The arm $\m{r}_0$ is fixed and
the rotational contribution is the tangent to the true circular path. It is
accurate for small rotations. It does not move the dependent node along the
finite-rotation arc.

For a rotation of magnitude $\theta$ about a fixed axis, the exact current
position is
\begin{equation}
    \m{x}_s=\m{x}_m+\m{R}(\theta)\m{r}_0,
    \label{rbe-finite-rotation}
\end{equation}
where $\m{R}$ is a finite rotation tensor. A geometrically nonlinear coupling
updates the constraint and its tangent during the equilibrium iterations. A
geometrically linear analysis retains Equation~\eqref{rbe-small-rotation}, even
if the resulting plotted rotation is visually large.

This distinction affects not only displacement. The current lever arm in
$\m{M}=\m{r}\times\m{F}$, follower directions, spring orientations and
remote reactions may all differ between small- and large-displacement
formulations. Large displacement should therefore be enabled whenever finite
rigid-body rotation materially changes geometry or load direction, even if the
strains remain small.

\begin{bbox}[0.94]
    \textbf{Common mistake -- interpreting the tangent as the rotated arm.}
    In a small-displacement solution, an RBE-connected node follows the initial
    tangent generated by the infinitesimal rotation term. It does not trace the
    finite circular trajectory. A 90-degree prescribed rotation is therefore
    outside the validity of the linearized RBE equation.
\end{bbox}

\subsection{Modal and Dynamic Analyses}

An exact RBE2 does not add elastic strain energy, but it removes deformation
modes from the connected patch. The resulting kinematic stiffening can raise
natural frequencies and alter mode shapes. It may also relocate attached mass
and inertia through the transformation matrix. RBE3 generally preserves local
flexibility, but the selected interpolation still determines how remote mass,
forces and moments participate in the modes.

A mass placed at a remote reference point requires particular care. Its
translational and rotary inertia are transferred through the coupling and may
produce physically intended or unintended inertial moments. Modal results should
be checked for local rigidization, unrealistic high-frequency modes and mass
participation changes.

\subsection{Interaction with Contact and Other Constraints}

An RBE is not a substitute for contact. Coupling an entire potential contact
surface rigidly can suppress its pressure deformation and create artificial
edge loading. Overlapping RBE2 spiders, bonded contact, symmetry conditions and
prescribed displacements may also impose duplicate relations on the same degrees
of freedom.

Constraint warnings should be investigated rather than automatically ignored.
A redundant set may be harmless in one enforcement method and singular in
another. It may also conceal a physically overconstrained joint even when the
solver successfully removes redundant equations.

\subsection{RBE3 Load Distribution Over a Surface}

An RBE3 distributes generalized nodal loads; it does not automatically apply a
uniform pressure. The distinction is easiest to see on the top face of a meshed
box. Interior nodes generally have a larger tributary area than edge nodes, and
corner nodes have the smallest tributary area.

Two weighting choices lead to different interpretations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item With \textbf{equal nodal weights}, every connected node may receive the
        same direct nodal force. Since tributary areas differ, the equivalent
        traction is larger at edge and corner nodes than in the interior.
    \item With \textbf{tributary-area weights}, interior nodes receive a larger
        nodal force, edge nodes a smaller force, and corner nodes the smallest
        force. This is the nodal pattern corresponding approximately to uniform
        pressure on a regular mesh.
\end{enumerate}

Thus the often observed smaller force at the edge is not an intrinsic property
of every RBE3; it results from an area-sensitive weighting or from the solver's
surface integration. If the purpose is to represent a physical pressure and
local stresses matter, applying pressure directly to the element faces is
normally preferable. An RBE3 is appropriate when the engineering quantity is a
resultant force and moment introduced through an attachment.

\subsection{Selecting the Spider Legs}

Connecting every node in a large surface is not automatically more accurate.
Engineers often select corner, perimeter or otherwise representative nodes for
three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The selected points already span the translations and rotations that
        the coupling must reproduce.
    \item A sparse spider gives a clear and auditable load path and avoids
        inadvertently involving flexible interior detail.
    \item Every additional connected degree of freedom increases the size and
        density of the constraint operator.
\end{enumerate}

For RBE2, adding more legs expands the region whose local deformation is
suppressed. For RBE3, it enlarges the interpolation and load-distribution
calculation. Depending on the solver's elimination and sparse-factorization
strategy, a very large spider can increase matrix fill-in, memory consumption,
constraint processing and solution time. Some input formats or solver modules
also impose explicit limits on the number of terms in one equation or coupling.

There is no universal optimum number of legs. The minimum requirement is a
geometrically non-degenerate set capable of representing the intended force and
moment components. Collinear nodes cannot robustly define all three rotations;
a small cluster may generate poor conditioning; and an excessively large patch
may distribute the load beyond the physical attachment. The selected topology
should follow the real load-transfer region rather than the mesh density.

\begin{bbox}[0.94]
    \textbf{Engineering note -- corner-node practice.} Corner-only spiders are
    common because corner nodes provide large lever arms and span the principal
    dimensions with few equations. They are not universally correct. A bearing
    seat, bolt-hole circumference or adhesive footprint should be represented
    by nodes that follow its actual load path, with weights chosen for the
    intended force distribution.
\end{bbox}

\section{Engineering Selection Guide}

Before selecting a coupling, answer the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is the omitted connection physically rigid relative to the retained
        structure? If yes, an RBE2 or rigid remote coupling may be appropriate.
    \item Must the connected surface retain local bending and warping? If yes,
        prefer an RBE3 or deformable remote coupling.
    \item Is the objective to apply a resultant force and moment, or to reproduce
        a distributed traction? Use an interpolation coupling for the former and
        a face pressure or traction for the latter.
    \item Does the connection possess finite stiffness or damping? Add a spring,
        bushing, beam, joint or explicit component; neither RBE2 nor RBE3 supplies
        that constitutive behaviour by itself.
    \item Can the interface separate or slide? Use the contact formulations of
        Chapter~\ref{chap:contact-formulations}.
    \item Are thermal expansion, large rotation, inertia or solver-specific
        limits important? Verify the chosen implementation rather than relying
        only on the generic RBE name.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.38\textwidth}|p{0.48\textwidth}}
    \textbf{Modelling objective} & \textbf{Typical choice} \\ \hline
    Perfectly rigid flange or insert & RBE2 or rigid remote coupling \\ 
    Introduce a resultant load without rigidizing a face & RBE3 or deformable remote coupling \\ 
    Flexible bearing or machine mount & RBE3 plus spring or bushing \\ 
    Uniform surface pressure & Element-face pressure, not equal-weight RBE3 \\ 
    Joint with finite beam-like stiffness & Beam or connector with suitable coupling \\ 
    Opening, sliding or intermittent interface & Contact \\ 
    Thermally expanding rigid attachment & Thermally enabled rigid coupling or explicit model \\ 
    Large rigid-body rotation & Geometrically nonlinear, finite-rotation coupling \\ 
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Summary}

Rigid elements are structured applications of the multifreedom-constraint theory
introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:multifreedom-constraints}. RBE1 provides a
general linear relationship, RBE2 enforces rigid-body motion from an independent
reference node, and RBE3 interpolates a reference motion from surrounding nodes
and distributes conjugate loads without rigidizing the connected patch. Remote
points are higher-level modelling objects that generate related solver entities.

The choice between these formulations is a choice about admissible deformation,
not merely about software syntax. RBE2 can introduce substantial kinematic
stiffening. RBE3 preserves surface flexibility but does not automatically create
a uniform pressure distribution and can become computationally expensive when
connected indiscriminately to very large node sets. Thermal expansion, finite
rotation, dynamics, contact and overlapping constraints all require additional
attention. A reliable model follows the physical load path, couples only the
necessary degrees of freedom and uses the simplest topology that reproduces the
intended resultant forces, moments and deformations.
